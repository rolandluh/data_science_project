## ABSTRACT

Roll-out plans for public Electric Vehicle Charging Infrastructure (EVCI) often rely on demand predictions to locate charging stations. However, we find that most existing prediction models are not well-designed to support a location decision, as they: (1) are constructed at spatially aggregated levels, (2) do not consider the longitudinal robustness of their predictions, and (3) are often based on observed charging demand without considering its limitations. In this study, we present a prediction model that is trained on real-world charging data from Brussels and discuss its relevant design parameters to support location planning. Our results demonstrate that even at very detailed spatial levels (e.g., building block level), prediction models possess significant predictive power. However, the predictive performance is largely determined by the metric that is used to measure demand. We compare the predictions with a unique dataset of georeferenced citizen's requests for charging stations, which demonstrates the limitations of solely using observed charging data to predict charging demand. We advise future research and practitioners to also consider the spatial coverage of the EVCI network, besides demand predictions, when deciding on the locations of new charging stations.

## 1. Introduction

Electric Vehicles (EVs) have a great potential to reduce the impact of climate change and improve local air quality. Research utilizing life cycle assessments to compare the environmental impact of different vehicle technologies has demonstrated that EVs have lower Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions compared to Internal Combustion Engine Vehicles (ICEVs) (Hoekstra, 2019; Van Mierlo et al., 2017). Even when considering the EU energy mix, the GHG emissions of EVs is about 17–30 % lower than the emissions of petrol and diesel cars (EEA, 2018). Besides the reduction of GHG emissions, EVs are also promising in terms of improving local air pollution (such as particulate matter (PM), nitrogen oxide (NOx)) and noise pollution. This is especially beneficial in large, densely populated urban areas, where high levels of these pollutants are being measured (EEA, 2020; Hooftman et al., 2018).

As a result, many cities have set ambitious targets regarding emission standards of passenger cars. Examples can be found in Europe, where several cities have already set targets for banning access to combustion engine vehicles (Bernard et al., 2021). More recently, Europe has voted a legislation to ban the sale of new petrol and diesel cars on the entire continent by 2035 (Klingert, 2022). As EVs are an appropriate alternative for ICEVs, the feasibility of these policy targets will largely depend on the market share that EVs will be able to attain.

However, for electric vehicles to be adopted by the vast majority of consumers, the availability of sufficient charging infrastructure is vital (Anderson et al., 2018). Previous research has demonstrated that home location chargers (i.e., where residential charging takes place) constitute the most important type of infrastructure to encourage the adoption of EVs (Hardman et al., 2018). This creates major challenges in urban areas, where a large share of residents is dependent on public Charging Stations (CS) for charging and where the available space is scarce (He et al., 2022; Namdeo et al., 2014). As the development of public Electric Vehicle Charging Infrastructure (EVCI) requires high investment costs, a well-thought roll-out strategy for locating public charging stations is fundamental. In addition, given that adoption of EVs varies in space (Chen et al., 2015; Morton et al., 2018; Mukherjee and Ryan, 2020) and time (Massiani and Gohs, 2015; Selena Sheng et al., 2022), this means that policy makers and urban planners need to decide both *where* and *when* public charging stations should be placed.

Roll-out plans for public EVCI usually involve two steps. In the first step, demand for public charging infrastructure is predicted and in the second step, charging stations are optimally located accordingly (Ko et al., 2017). While the body of literature within both fields is growing, we find that a discrepancy exists between them. That is, existing prediction models are not properly adapted to the strategic and long-term location decision for which they are used in the second stage. First, most existing models predict charging demand on aggregated spatial units (e.g. municipalities, or regions), which lack the sufficient level of detail to support a location decision. Second, studies do not consider the longitudinal robustness of demand predictions. Once installed, charging stations are fixed to their locations, meaning that demand predictions should not only consider current- but also future demand. Third, existing models are validated by comparing observed charging demand (at existing CSs) with their predictions. However, this observed demand is an imperfect measure of the true underlying demand (Hüttel et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2022; Weekx et al., 2024), which poses the question how well the models align with the true demand and what role they should take in EVCI location planning.

Therefore, we aim to bridge this gap in literature by answering the following research question: *"What is the impact of the spatial, temporal, and metric dimension on predicting charging demand to support public charging infrastructure location planning?"*. We present a model that predicts EV charging demand for public CSs on different levels of spatial detail (spatial dimension), over multiple years (temporal dimension), and for multiple demand metrics (metric dimension). Our model is trained on real-world charging data from public charging stations in the Brussels Capital Region, together with socio-economic, mobility, and land-use data. We furthermore compare the model's predictions with a unique dataset of geo-referenced 'user requests'. These requests stem from Brussels' citizens who ask for a charging station in their neighborhood via an online form, and thus complement observed charging data. This new data source is cross validated with the prediction model, allowing for a new way of validating the predicted demand. [*Margin comment in PDF next to this passage: "validation idea!"*]

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 provides a literature review on the existing methods to predict EV charging demand, including the contributions of this study. The case study and the overall model design is presented in Section 3, followed by an elaboration on the material and methods in Section 4. Results and discussion are given in Section 5 and Section 6 respectively, and the conclusion is given in Section 7.

### 2.1. Methods for demand prediction

#### 2.1.1. Regression modelling

Regression Modelling (RM) has been used to predict demand for EVs based on various socio-economic, land-use and transport related data. Demand is proxied by either existing EV adoption (Chen et al., 2015; Morton et al., 2018; Mukherjee and Ryan, 2020; Selena Sheng et al., 2022), parking duration (Chen et al., 2013), or variables related to the usage/location of existing charging stations (Dong et al., 2019; He et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2022). The main advantage of RM is its ability to analyze the spatial patterns inherent to the observed demand. By incorporating distance and/−or neighborhood matrices, these studies can report on spatial heterogeneity and autocorrelation. Existing studies have documented the importance of considering the spatial effect in EV demand. Chen et al. (2015), Morton et al. (2018) and Mukherjee and Ryan (2020) found evidence for the significant presence of neighborhood effects in terms of EV adoption (i.e., the level of adoption in a neighboring area is positively associated with EV adoption). Selena Sheng et al. (2022) find that the level of EV infrastructure in neighboring areas positively and significantly impacts EV adoption. When analyzing the count of the number of charging points per km² grid cell, Dong et al. (2019) found no evidence for the presence of spatial dependency. Finally, Kang et al. (2022) find the presence of spatial heterogeneity in their regression model on charging demand indicators.

RM models are typically built for one specific city or country. Hence, the resulting predictions from these models vary largely in terms of spatial resolution, going from regions with an average size of about 1 km² (Cai et al., 2014; He et al., 2022) to 600 km² (Morton et al., 2018). Regarding the temporal dimension, only Kang et al. (2022) incorporate time in their model by separating it for weekdays and weekends, and Selena Sheng et al. (2022) include the adoption rates from the previous year as explanatory variable. However, a clear validation of whether demand predictions are valid over time is lacking.

#### 2.1.2. Simulation modelling

A large part of studies within Simulation Modelling (SM) utilizes trajectory data (stemming from GPS devices or travel surveys) on ICEVs to predict the hypothetical charging demand of EVs. Demand predictions are expressed either in energy consumption (X. Li and Jenn, 2022; Wang et al., 2019), parking hours (Cai et al., 2014), or distance travelled (Hardinghaus et al., 2019). The former is typically retrieved from energy consumption estimation models, while the latter two metrics are obtained from probability density estimation models or through specific ad-hoc frameworks (incorporating a variety of methods such as traffic models, human behavior decision-making models, etc.). The main advantage of simulation models based on trajectory data is that predictions can be made on a relatively detailed level of spatial and temporal resolution. As trajectories typically include timestamps and coordinates of arrival and departure, demand can be mapped intraday over a set of discrete points in space. However, these methods are not able to provide predictions over multiple years, and the quality of the prediction is largely dependent on the quality and representativeness of trajectory data. Another part of SM includes Agent-Based Models (ABM) or similar ad-hoc simulation logic. ABMs model the behavior of autonomous agents within a specific area and period. The decisions made by the agents are based on sets of rules, which are learnt from the input data and the assumptions that the model is built on (J. Li et al., 2021). Gnann et al. (2019) built an ABM that incorporates (among others) the decision of each agent to purchase an EV. Combined with mobility survey data and the existing EVCI, this allows the model to predict the kWh charged at these stations. Similarly, Wolbertus et al. (2021) suggest an ABM in which real-world historical charging data is used to derive individual charging profiles, which are subsequently used as decision rules by the agents to simulate their charging behavior. Weekx et al. (2024) also rely on charging data to simulate how many additional charging stations are needed when the demand in the network is expanded. Although ABMs can incorporate a detailed spatial resolution and multiple time periods, the way in which the decision rules are assigned to the agents largely determines the outcome of the model. As these decision rules are difficult to validate, the question remains how accurate the predictions of these ABMs are.

#### 2.1.3. Data mining

[*Margin comment in PDF next to this heading: "The ML approach"*]

With gradually more data on EVs becoming available, Data Mining (DM) is being used to predict charging demand from historical charging datasets. DM models apply supervised machine learning to predict the popularity of existing charging stations, expressed in either kWh consumed at the CS (Hecht et al., 2024), CS utilization (Mortimer et al., 2022; Pevec et al., 2018; Wagner et al., 2014), the number of unique visitors at the station (Straka et al., 2020), or a hybrid combination of these metrics (Wolbertus, 2024). Existing research has found the popularity of charging stations to be moderately predictable. Wolbertus (2024) finds that predictive performance depends on the popularity metric that is used (they report R² values between 10 and 30 % using linear regression). Similar findings are reported by Wagner et al. (2014) who find a R² of 32 % when predicting charging point utilization using a Beta regression model, and Mortimer et al. (2022) who report a R² of 18 % for a linear regression model. Hecht et al. (2024) predict the average daily energy consumption of CSs in Germany and report a R² of 61 %. This demonstrates the potential of using more advanced machine-learning algorithms. Straka et al. (2020) use a classification model to predict the popularity of a charging station as belonging to the top 25 % (in terms of unique visitors). The authors report a mean AUC value of 89 % based on training a Logistic regression model. While these studies demonstrate the potential of data-driven prediction models, existing models have not yet analyzed how model performance is dependent on the spatial level of detail on which predictions are made, which would be of interest to urban planners when optimizing CS locations. Regarding the temporal dimension, existing DM models do not differentiate predictions over multiple years or consider the robustness of their predictions over time. #### 2.1.4. Multi-criteria analysis

When EV adoption rates are still low and less data is available, Multi-Criteria Analysis (MCA) can be a good alternative due to their low data requirements. In MCA, demand is predicted by combining multiple variables that are hypothesized to influence demand, often using GIS tools (Grote et al., 2019). To combine all variables, existing studies have applied weighted linear combination (Costa et al., 2019; Gkatzoflias et al., 2016; Namdeo et al., 2014), analytical hierarchy process (Pucci, 2021), or specific ad-hoc frameworks (based on weighted combinations or overlapping areas) (Csiszár et al., 2019; Grote et al., 2019). As the input variables typically have different units and levels of spatial detail, the resulting demand measure is a relative, unitless score. Hence, the results are particularly useful when the goal is to mutually discriminate between regions. It is apparent from Table 1 that most MCA studies map demand on a detailed spatial level (often less than 1 km²), however while also neglecting the temporal dimension. Furthermore, these studies are more useful to determine the initial locations where a limited set of charging stations should be installed (Grote et al., 2019).

#### 2.1.5. Diffusion modeling

Diffusion Models (DIFF) have been used to predict EV adoption over time. Diffusion models represent the adoption of new technology in time using a sigmoidal adoption curve (Rogers, 2010). Multiple models can be used to fit the sigmoidal curve. Some commonly used methods are the logistic model, Gompertz model, and the Bass model, with the last being the most used in EV literature. DIFF models are well able to predict over long time periods (10–30 years), and can be applied to cities (Lee et al., 2019), countries (Massiani and Gohs, 2015), or even continents (Decock et al., 2020). However, accurately finding the parameters to fit a diffusion model is difficult, and research has shown that results can vary widely with small changes in the estimated parameters (Massiani and Gohs, 2015). Furthermore, DIFF models do not predict the spatial distribution of adoption within these regions (i.e., no spatial resolution is given).

### 2.2. Research gap: Predicting demand to support location planning

Table 1 demonstrates that the existing models in literature have a wide variety in terms of their ability to predict in space and time, as well as how charging demand is measured. It is notable that the temporal dimension is lacking for most methods. Only the agent-based SM models are typically able to produce predictions on a longer horizon, however, also relying on strong and difficult to validate assumptions related to the agent's decision rules. DIFF methods on the other hand provide a solution to forecast over multiple years, however, with a high degree of uncertainty and no ability to discriminate predictions over space. The spatial dimension is considered by most methods, however each with a different focus. RM have a focus on identifying neighborhood effects in EV adoption, while MCA studies take a wide variety of geo-spatial variables into account. SM models in turn map charging demand to exact locations based on the detailed trajectory- or charging data they use as input. A drawback of these studies is their lack of properly validating the demand predictions. This limitation is partly addressed by DM models, that can produce predictions on a detailed spatial level, and by splitting data into training-, validation-, and test sets, also validate them with real-world charging behavior.

However, for demand predictions to be useful in the roll-out planning of EVCI, it is important that they are: (1) reliable on a spatial level of detail that is sufficiently fine-grained to make a location decision; (2) reliable over time, as installing public charging stations requires significant resources (Tsiropoulos et al., 2022) and thus is a strategic-long term decision; and (3) be a valid measure of the true, underlying demand for public charging stations. In this sense, we find a gap in literature on these three aspects. First, the models proposed by existing studies largely vary in terms of their spatial level of detail. No research has yet explored whether an optimal level of spatial granularity for EV demand predictions exists, and how this influences model performance. Second, Table 1 demonstrated that most existing studies lack a clear temporal dimension. Predictions are made for a discrete point in time, however, without considering their robustness over time. Third, when existing models are validated, this is generally done using observed charging data, which is an imperfect measure of the true underlying demand (Kang et al., 2022). On the one hand, the charging demand can only be observed at locations where existing stations are installed. On the other hand, at those locations where infrastructure was available, the measured demand is censored (Hüttel et al., 2023; Weekx et al., 2024) by the limited capacity of a public charging station (usually two plugs).

### 2.3. Contributions of this study

In this study, we present a model that is specifically designed and validated to predict EV charging demand for public CSs in support of EVCI location planning. We use a DM model based on a real-world dataset of public charging stations in Brussels as it can make predictions on a detailed spatial level while also allowing for validation on a separate hold-out set. In line with the research gaps identified in Section 2.2, our research contributes to the existing literature on three points:

1. We train and test the model on different levels of spatial granularity. This allows to analyze the trade-off between model accuracy (i.e., level of spatial detail) and model performance, as well as to test whether an optimal level of spatial detail exists.
2. We test the longitudinal robustness of the model's demand predictions by training the model with charging data on year *t*, and subsequently testing it with data on year *t* + 1. This model design has not yet been considered in existing research, while it would be more relevant to support in strategic (and thus long-term) location decisions.
3. We compare our model's demand predictions with a unique dataset of geo-referenced 'user requests' for charging stations. These requests were made by citizens of Brussels who requested a public charging station in their neighborhood through an online form. This dataset can alleviate the issues of bias in observed charging demand (i.e., see Section 2.2) and allows for an independent comparison of the model's predictions.

## 3. Case study & design

The Brussels Capital Region (in this study also referred to as 'Brussels') is used as case study for the prediction model. Brussels has a population of 1.2 million citizens, an area of 161 km², and is geographically organized in different spatial levels: 118 districts, 724 census tracts, and 5557 building blocks. Fig. 1 shows each of these levels on a map. Brussels is a relevant use case as it has set ambitious targets for restricting access to combustion engine passenger cars (Brussel Leefmilieu, 2020). As a result, the development of a well-thought EVCI roll-out strategy is essential to support the mass EV adoption that is expected over the next decade.

The design of the prediction model is presented by the conceptual model in Fig. 2. The prediction model uses spatial data to predict charging demand for public EVCI, and always distincts between a training set that is used to train the model's (hyper-)parameters and a separate test set used to measure the model's performance. Hence, the model is characterized by three dimensions:

$$f_{s,t,m} \qquad s \in S,\; t \in T,\; m \in M$$

With *f* indicating the prediction model, *S* the set of spatial levels, *T* the set of temporal levels, and *M* the set of metrics. First, the spatial dimension (*S*) determines the level of detail (i.e., accuracy) on which predictions are made. Four spatial levels are considered,

$$S = \{\text{district, buffer 400 m, census tract, building block}\}$$

which are presented in Table 2. The buffer 400 m level predicts the demand at a given location, based on all variables measured within a radius of 400 m around that location (as shown by the blue circles in Fig. 1). This buffer method is commonly used when dealing with georeferenced data (Straka and Buzna, 2019). A distance of 400 m is chosen, as this approximates the maximum range that EV drivers are willing to walk to find a charging station (Weekx et al., 2024).

Second, the temporal dimension (*T*) determines which data is used for training and testing the model, which is depicted in Fig. 3. Given the availability of the charging data, the following train-test combinations are considered:

$$T = \{2021\text{–}2022,\; 2022\text{–}2023,\; 2021\text{–}2023\}$$

All stations that were fully available in a given year *t* (i.e., installed before Jan. 1st of year *t* and thus operating for all 365 days) are used for training (blue circles in Fig. 3), and all stations fully available in the next year are used for testing (blue circles and green triangles in Fig. 3). Note that this implies that the same stations that were operating in the training set in year *t* are also included in the test set in year *t* + 1, however, with their newly registered demand for *t* + 1. This modelling choice was made as it allows to verify to what extent the observed demand of a charging station in year *t* is a good predictor for its future demand in year *t* + 1. As new public charging stations were continuously installed over time, the number of stations on which the model is trained & tested differs for each year (which is also shown in the maps in Fig. 3). By only including fully available stations, an equal comparison between the different charging stations in the train and test set is possible. Furthermore, by training and testing the models on different years, we better resemble the realistic setting of predicting *future* charging demand, which has scarcely been considered in previous research.

Third, the metric dimension (*M*) determines how charging demand is measured. Based on the metrics used by previous studies (Wolbertus, 2024), four metrics are calculated,

$$M = \{\text{duration, energy, sessions, users}\}$$

which are presented in Table 3. Each of the metrics is measured for each temporal-, and spatial level. Regarding the latter, this means that the metrics should be properly mapped to each spatial level. The mapping function depends on the level of spatial detail, as indicated by Table 2. For the district- and census tract level this is done by taking the mean of all stations in each feature. For the building block level, each charging station is linked to its closest building block (and the mean of all stations is used when multiple stations link to the same building block). In case of the buffer 400 m model, each buffer is linked to exactly one station. When combining all dimensions, 48 (= |S| × |T| × |M| = 4 × 3 × 4) model versions are considered in total.

## 4. Material & methods

The process of building a prediction model involves several structured steps (Provost and Fawcett, 2013), which are elaborated in the three subsections here below. Section 4.1 describes all datasets used in the model (see Fig. 2), and which data cleaning- and pre-processing steps were undertaken. Section 4.2 elaborates on the algorithm that is used to fit the model to the data, while Section 4.3 discusses how the model is validated.

### 4.1. Data collection & pre-processing

This study is based on three datasets, of which the data-collection and pre-processing steps are described here below. The spatial data (Section 4.1.1) is used as exogeneous input to predict charging demand, which is measured from the charging events (Section 4.1.2). Finally, the predictions are compared with the user requests (Section 4.1.2).

#### 4.1.1. Spatial data

Geo-referenced variables are used as predictors to train the prediction model. Table 4 gives an overview of all 18 variables included in this study. The explanatory variables are grouped as measuring either the land-use, socio-economic, or mobility characteristics of an area (Weekx et al., 2023). Each variable is collected on a given level of spatial detail, which is indicated by the 'spatial level' column. As the prediction model is built on different spatial levels (see Table 2 for an overview), some variables need to be mapped (i.e., aggregated or disaggregated) to the appropriate level. How this mapping is done depends on the measurement unit of the variable (Straka and Buzna, 2019). The column 'spatial mapping' in Table 4 indicates the mapping function that is used for each variable. All variables that measure count data (e.g., off-street parking, salary cars, etc.) are mapped using a weighted sum function. This function assumes that the count variable is uniformly distributed over each feature's area. Variables that are measured in a relative unit are mapped using the weighted mean function, which assumes that the value of the variable in the new spatial level is proportional to the relative area of each feature of the original variable in the new spatial level. Both methods are visually demonstrated in Fig. 4. Regarding the temporal level, the predictor set remains constant over all model versions. This also reflects the fact that on the short term all variables in Table 4 remain constant. All data is furthermore pre-processed depending on the variable's distribution. Variables with extreme outliers are truncated, while skewed distributions are log transformed. This is indicated in the 'transformation' column in Table 4.

#### 4.1.2. Charging events

Charging demand is measured via historically observed charging events at public charging stations in Brussels. Each charging event corresponds to an EV driver plugging in its vehicle at the station, and contains attributes such as the start time, end time, energy consumption (kWh), unique ID of the station, and unique ID of the EV driver (based on the RFID card that was used to initiate the session). Three years of charging data is available, from Jan. 1st, 2021, up until Dec. 31st, 2023. The charging events are cleaned before they are used for calculating the demand metrics. First, sessions that are technically impossible (e.g., power level higher than power level of charging station) are omitted from the dataset. Second charging sessions with a duration of less than 2 min or more than 24 h are removed. The former is done to exclude the impact of faulty transactions from EV drivers, which can skew the sessions metric. The latter is similarly done to reduce the impact of extremely long charging sessions on the duration metric.

#### 4.1.3. User requests

In 2023, the Brussels government launched the initiative 'Electrify Brussels', an online platform to inform about the network of electric vehicle charging points in Brussels (Brussels Environment, 2024). This platform includes an online form that allows citizens to request a charging station in their neighborhood. Requests can be made by anyone, regardless of whether they own an EV or not. All requests are gathered as of the form's launch on Sep. 29, 2023, up until Apr. 25, 2024, and includes a timestamp and requested location (street and number). After removing duplicate requests, a total of 396 requests are collected. The number of user requests that are made in a certain area are considered as a proxy for charging demand in that area, which is used to externally validate the results of the prediction model. It should be noted however that, as any metric, this metric also has its limitations. As some citizens may not be aware or do not find their way to the online form, the lack of user requests in a certain area does not necessarily correspond to no charging demand in that area. However, the areas with a higher number of user requests can be considered in high demand for public charging.

### 4.2. Model building

The prediction model is constructed for each level of the spatial- (*S*), temporal- (*T*), and metric- (*M*) dimension (see Section 3). The combination of the spatial and temporal level of the model determines the number of observations that are available for training and testing, which is presented in Table 5. As more charging stations are installed over time, the number of observations increases with the temporal level. Similarly, as aggregated spatial levels consider larger areas and group more charging stations within their region, the number of observations increases with the level of spatial detail. The set of predictors that is used to train the model remains constant for all versions and consists of the 18 features presented in Table 4.

Each prediction model is designed as a classification problem, of which the goal is to predict whether a given spatial area belongs to the top *q*ₘ% in terms of the demand metrics *m* ∈ *M*. Hence, the resulting outcome variable is expressed as the class probability of belonging to the top *q*ₘ%. The decision to model demand as a binary variable is made for several reasons. First, when predicting charging demand to support a location decision, we argue that the focus should be to discriminate between different regions, rather than to provide an exact estimate of a stations' energy consumption or utilization. Previous studies have furthermore shown that such models possess low predictive performance (Mortimer et al., 2022; Wagner et al., 2014; Wolbertus, 2024), which is not surprising given the limited size of current public charging networks (in terms of number of stations) and the early adoption phase of EVs. Classification models can discriminate between regions by estimating the class probability of each observation. Second, the size of the training sets (see Table 5) is relatively small compared to the number of predictors (see Table 4), which classification methods can handle well (Straka et al., 2020). Third, fast developments in EV adoption and battery technology (Van Mierlo et al., 2021) make that the observed demand at charging stations increases over time. Using the relative metric of top *q*ₘ% charging stations mitigates the effect of non-stationary charging demand over time. As a result, this also implies that our model should be used to identify regions that are optimal for installing charging stations, rather than to determine the optimal sizing of the infrastructure. We specify *q*ₘ = 20 %, ∀*m* ∈ *M* in this study, which is similar to what has been used in previous research (Straka et al., 2020).

A Random Forest (RF) classification model is used in this study. RF is an ensemble machine learning technique which uses the results from multiple independent decision trees to vote for a class prediction (Breiman, 2001). Multiple trees are generated by randomly sampling from the training data with replacement (i.e., bagging). When looking for the optimal split, each tree only uses a random subset of features, which improves the model's robustness. Previous research has furthermore reported good predictive performance of this method (Friese et al., 2021; Straka et al., 2020). The number of trees that the RF model is allowed to generate (λ) is a critical hyperparameter that determines the model's performance. To avoid overfitting, stratified k-fold cross validation is used to select the optimal value of λ that balances model accuracy with generalization. This is done for *k* = 5 folds and considering the values of λ in the range of {10, 20, ⋯, 250}. The value of λ that resulted in the highest mean model performance over all folds is chosen, after which the model is re-trained on all training data and performance is tested on the test set. To remove any resampling bias, this process of finding the optimal λ and measuring performance is repeated 50 times, which also allows to report on the level of confidence of the prediction model. The model is implemented in Python using the scikit-learn package.

### 4.3. Model validation

Model performance is measured based on the Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC) graph. This method calculates the performance of a classification model, by constructing a plot with false positive rate on the x axis, and true positive rate on the y axis. A point on the ROC curve is superior to another if it lies to the northwest of that point. ROC graphs are a common method to measure model performance and have the advantage of being independent of the class proportions in the target variable (unlike e.g., accuracy) (Provost and Fawcett, 2013). The Area Under the ROC curve (AUC) is used to obtain one summary statistic of each model's ROC graph. The AUC ranges between 0 and 1, where a value of 0.5 corresponds to a random guessing model, while a value 1 represents a model that perfectly distinguishes all positives and negatives in the target variable.

The performance of each model is benchmarked in two ways. First, the AUC achieved on the test set is compared to the AUC achieved by a baseline model that randomly guesses the class probability for each instance in the same test set. This approach allows to validate for each model whether it has statistically significant predictive power. Second, the predicted class probability of each instance is compared with the count of user requests for that same instance. This allows for an independent validation of the model's performance and verifies whether a roll-out that follows demand predictions would also adhere to the stated needs of EV drivers.

#### 5.1.2. Spatial performance

Fig. 5 shows that the models on the district level achieve high predictive performance compared to their comparable models on the other spatial levels. #### 5.1.3. Metric performance

Fig. 6 demonstrates that model performance is heavily determined by the metric that is used to measure charging demand, which was also found by previous research (Wolbertus, 2024). The choice of metric has a higher impact than the spatial level on which predictions are made. The highest performance is consistently achieved by the users metric, with a mean AUC of 85 %. This may be explained by the fact that this is the most aggregate metric to measure station popularity (i.e., the number of unique users is independent of the number of sessions, their duration, and energy consumption). Next follows the sessions and energy models, which have a mean AUC of 74 % and 71 % respectively. The lowest model performance is achieved from the duration models, with a mean AUC of 64 %. This may be due to the large variation that is inherent to this metric, as the duration of a charging session can differ considerably between sessions (i.e., daytime versus nighttime session). These findings are furthermore consistent with the results of previous research that found high predictive performance for estimating the number of unique users at a charging stations (Straka et al., 2020; Wolbertus, 2024), and low performance when estimating a station's utilization (i.e., connection duration) (Mortimer et al., 2022).

However, as mentioned in Section 4.1.2, the lack of user requests in a given area does not necessarily correspond to no charging demand. It is expected that as the number of user requests increases, the predicted demand score also increases. Hence, each scatterplot furthermore shows a regression line, which is colored green if a significant positive Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was found between both variables and gray otherwise.

## 6. Discussion

This study has demonstrated that designing a prediction model to support EVCI location planning is a multi-dimensional problem. We shed light on the model's spatial, temporal and metric dimensions. The optimal design of the model will depend on the final goal for which the model will be used, which is often neglected in existing studies.

### 6.1. Recommendations for EVCI prediction modelling

First, our findings suggest that the level of spatial detail on which predictions are made has relatively low impact on the model's predictive performance. For each spatial level of detail considered in this study, the predictive model was found to significantly perform better than its random guessing baseline model. This means that demand prediction models can be useful for EVCI location planning, both on a strategic- as well as an operational level. On the strategic level, more aggregate predictions (e.g. at district level) can be used to support long-term decisions, such as where first to reinforce the electricity grid for future installments of charging stations. However, on the operational level, predictions on a detailed spatial level (e.g. at building block level) can be used to identify the exact locations where charging stations should be installed.

Second, we find that the observed charging demand in year *t* can be used in combination with spatial data to predict demand in year *t* + 1 and *t* + 2. This indicates the relevance using demand predictions in the locations planning of EVCI. Furthermore, as more charging stations are installed over time, more charging data becomes available to train the model, which could further improve model performance. Hence, practitioners could explicitly focus developing their initial EVCI network in a coverage-based manner and re-calibrate the prediction models over time. This is especially relevant for models that predict consumed energy and connection duration, as more room for an increase in predictive performance is available.

Third, the choice of metric to measure charging demand has a significant impact on predictive performance and should be aligned with the underlying goal of the EVCI network. The number of unique users, and the number of charging sessions at a station were respectively found to be the most predictable metrics. However, these demand metrics will also favor those locations with a high vehicle rotation (i.e., many different visitors for relatively short charging durations), and the prediction model will be inherently trained in recognizing such locations. As a result, an EVCI network that is solely focused on such demand predictions would neglect the needs of other user groups that have different charging habits (Weekx et al., 2025). Models that are trained on predicting locations where high amounts of energy volumes are consumed achieve slightly lower predictive performance. Nonetheless, their predictions will contribute to attract Charging Point Operators (CPOs) to install infrastructure, as the consumed energy highly determines the business model of operating the station. Finally, the models that are trained on identifying the most utilized charging stations (i.e., duration) achieve the lowest predictive performance. However, previous research has demonstrated the relationship between a station's observed utilization rate and its number of failed connected attempts (i.e., EV drivers that want to plug-in at the station but find it already fully occupied) (Helmus et al., 2022; Weekx et al., 2024). Hence, such models might be better in serving the overall level of convenience for EV drivers despite their lower predictive performance.

### 6.2. Recommendations for EVCI roll-out planning

We advise model builders and policy makers to not evaluate prediction models solely based on achieving a maximal predictive performance (e.g., AUC, R², MAE, …), but rather to consider all relevant design parameters that align with their EVCI location strategy. For the case of Brussels, a spatial analysis of user requests for charging stations revealed a considerable number of requests at remote locations from existing infrastructure. This highlights the importance of still incorporating spatial coverage in roll-out models, even when an existing network of charging stations has already been installed. This also opens pathways for future research to explore hybrid location strategies, where the location decisions are optimized with respect to both coverage and demand predictions.

When cross validating the results of the prediction model with the geo-referenced locations of user requests, we find low correlations between both datasets. This indicates that observed charging transactions, despite their promising value for data-driven research, should still be considered as an imperfect proxy to measure the true underlying charging demand. This shortcoming is visually represented in Fig. 11. Model builders and policy makers should consider this when using data-driven predictions in their EVCI roll-out plans, especially when the EVCI network is still under development, and a limited number of charging stations (i.e., data points) is available to measure demand. As such, we recommend that the initial location strategy of a charging network focusses on achieving as much spatial coverage as possible (De Clerck and Vanhaverbeke, 2021), which can subsequently support a more data-driven roll out.

Combining multiple data sources in EVCI roll-out plans can furthermore mitigate the shortcomings of solely using observed charging data. Regions with both low predicted charging demand and no user requests provide stronger support for a lack of true charging demand, whereas the opposite provides stronger evidence for high true charging demand. This research has demonstrated the potential of user requests, however other examples can be found in literature as well. For instance, Kang et al. (2022) measure charging demand as the location where EV drivers request directions to a public charging station in their smartphone app, which the authors consider as locations with 'unmet' charging demand. Such data sources, in combination with observed demand, are expected to provide a better overall overview of the true demand for public charging.

## 7. Conclusion

Public charging demand predictions constitute an important element in EVCI roll-out plans. However, existing research predicts charging demand at spatially aggregated levels, does not consider the longitudinal robustness of their predictions, and is mostly based on observed charging demand without considering its limitations. This raises the question how demand prediction models can be better designed to support location planning. Based on a real-world dataset of public charging stations in Brussels, we empirically estimate the effect of a model's spatial, temporal, and metric dimension on its predictive performance. We furthermore use a unique dataset of geo-referenced user requests to externally validate the prediction model.

This study's contributions can be summarized in four points. First, the spatial level of detail on which predictions are made has little influence on predictive performance. This indicates that when faced with a location decision, urban planners and location modelers can reliably use detailed demand prediction models (e.g., at building block level, with a spatial accuracy of about 0.01 km²) to discriminate between potential candidate regions to install charging infrastructure. Second, we find that charging demand predictions are robust over time, and the observed charging demand in one year can be used to predict the demand in the next year. This demonstrates the relevance of using demand predictions in the location planning of EVCI. Third, model performance is largely determined by the metric that is used to measure demand. Each metric has its own focus, and we recommend that practitioners do not decide on the optimal metric based on maximizing predictive performance, but rather based on the goals of the EVCI network. The sessions and unique users metric will favor locations with a high vehicle rotation, while the energy and duration metric focus on the CPO and the EV driver's level of convenience respectively. Fourth, comparing the demand prediction models with the user requests highlights the limitations of building models based on observed charging data. User requests were found at remote locations from the existing infrastructure, where no stations are available to measure demand. We recommend that EVCI location models are not solely data-driven, but also consider spatial coverage when determining new locations, which can alleviate this issue.

This study has several limitations. First, observed charging data was analyzed during the period of Jan 1st, 2021, up until Dec 31st, 2023, and may be affected by COVID-19 related restrictions (van der Koogh et al., 2023). This effect is not considered in this study, nor can a comparison be made before this period as no charging data was available at this time. However, as the charging network was gradually expanded and charging demand was measured in a relative way (i.e., a binary variable indicating the top 20 %), we expect the effect to be small. Second, this study is based on the Brussels Capital Region, which is a densely populated area where many vehicle owners and visitors rely on on-street parking. The results of this work may be different for other regions; however, the methods are transferable. Third it should be noted that the predictive model in this study could be refined to further improve predictive performance. Possible extensions could be to look at the use of neural networks or to incorporate the effect of spatial correlation across different spatial levels. Future work could explore how novel datasets, such as proposed in the work of Kang et al. (2022), can complement observed charging data in better grasping the true public charging demand. It would furthermore be interesting for location modelers to look at hybrid approaches of EVCI optimization, where both coverage and demand predictions are maximized.
